TLDR

We ran three independent repeats of Experiment 1 from WSP with Astra and DeepSeek, then examined how they recognized the games. GPT-6 Astra (gpt-6-astra, high) scored 100% with 1,730 output tokens per answer. DeepSeek Flash (deepseek-flash, max) scored 58.3% with 68,342 tokens, about 39.5 times as many. Visualizing the weights reveals why Astra succeeds: humans can read some of this structure too.

We keep the original architecture, observations, actions, weights and prompt from WSP, add DeepSeek as a comparison, and run three independent attempts per model and game. Each response receives all 17,253 weights, without tools.

Astra gets 12/12 right; DeepSeek gets 7/12. Both identify Breakout in all three attempts. Mean output use is 1,730 versus 68,342 tokens: a 39.5-fold difference.

Of DeepSeek’s five errors, three are Sokoban guesses, one is Snake and one is Asterix. In its first Pac-Man answer, it reconstructs a symmetric maze but names Asterix with 88% confidence. Even after recovering a game’s structure, a model can still name the wrong game.

In its final answers, DeepSeek devotes long passages to interpreting each channel and comparing candidate games. It weighs Sokoban, Snake, Pac-Man and road-crossing hypotheses against the observed weights. Sometimes the explanation drifts: for Freeway, it interprets moving objects as boxes, the top reward region as box destinations, and an upward action preference as pushing boxes toward those goals. It then picks Sokoban with 94% confidence.

Astra identifies more discriminating evidence: horizontal traffic lanes, weight patterns repeating every four columns, and a reward strip at the top. Together these point to a road-crossing game. DeepSeek reads the weights too, but often settles on broad descriptions—player, objects, goals and obstacles—that fit many games, then fills in the details of its chosen hypothesis. A longer, more complete story can still lead to the wrong answer.

This is not magic

With only the weights and no tools, a model can infer what a policy does and what world it operates in? That sounds extraordinary. How does it work? In this experimental setup, the weights are already less of a black box than they might seem. Humans can read considerable structure from them too.

Let’s start with how Experiment 1 works: given the weights of a small neural network trained by the authors with PPO, identify the game it plays. The weights go directly into the context. No tools are allowed; the model answers directly.

That sounds difficult. But the authors’ actual setup brings it down to the level of a toy question. Let’s look at the prompt.

As the illustration shows, the prompt explains six parts of the task. The first, observation, tells the model that the game is a 10 × 10 grid world. Its five channels encode occupancy for agent, moving, reward, obstacle and previous. In a frame of Breakout, for example, the agent channel contains the little paddle at the bottom; moving contains the ball; reward contains the bricks above; and obstacle contains the surrounding walls. Finally, previous records the preceding frame.

You may already see where this leads. Once we know the observation layout and that the policy is a simple fully connected network with two hidden layers, we can immediately form a hypothesis about its first-layer weights, provided the network is simple enough. For example, the relevant weights in the W matrix receiving the agent channel must lie along the bottom: the agent moves only there, while the rest of that channel stays unchanged throughout the game. Similarly, the weights receiving the reward channel should tend to concentrate near the top.

The actions also give us a hypothesis about the final layer. We would expect the weights associated with left and right to stand out, because the paddle cannot move up or down in Breakout.

So this is a toy-level setting. With basic visualization, humans can see and explain the weights. This is quite different from the black-box weights of the large models we usually discuss. The enormous scale is what makes interpretation difficult. For a small network, we can explain a great deal: it is, after all, a composite function.

All 160 first-layer maps

Each square in these plots represents the connection weight from one input position to one hidden unit. The first layer splits into five channels × 32 hidden units, giving 160 matrices of size 10 × 10. The figure above selects units actually cited in the answer; the complete atlas is in the expandable section.

The first-layer weights retain clear spatial structure: the paddle lies on the bottom row, reward targets sit above, and the ball can move through the field. The output layer emphasizes left and right. The model combines these clues into “Breakout.”

From another angle, the information in this question is incomplete: in principle, a policy network’s weights alone are not enough to uniquely identify its game. The model selects the most likely interpretation from its prior knowledge of familiar small games.

A more fully specified weight-analysis problem?

A natural next question is whether we can design a more determinate task, where a given set of weights has a definite answer, rather than several plausible game interpretations. We therefore designed tasks that ask language models to analyze mathematical properties directly from weights, without tools. That deserves its own article.
